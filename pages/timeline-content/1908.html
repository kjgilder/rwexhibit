Roger Williams University entered its final chapter, characterized by a renewed sense of Black autonomy followed by insurmountable institutional pressures. The relocation to the White’s Creek Pike campus across the Cumberland River was a symbolic and practical victory for the Tennessee Negro Baptist Association. Under the leadership of Reverend William Haynes, the university reopened on Emancipation Day, January 1, 1908, with a predominantly Black Board of Trustees, signaling a move toward self-determination and away from northern missionary oversight.

The Final Years

Despite the determination of its leaders, the 1920s brought severe challenges. The university struggled with financial pressures, internal denominational divisions, and competition from better-funded state and private institutions. Additionally, the loss of consistent support from the American Baptist Home Mission Society (ABHMS) and the lack of long-term leadership weakened the institution.

1929 Merger and Closure

In December 1929, the independent era of Roger Williams University ended when it merged with the Howe Institute in Memphis. The remaining students and faculty moved to Memphis, and the institution eventually became part of what is now LeMoyne-Owen College.
