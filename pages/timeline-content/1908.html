Educational Impact and Notable Alumni

After 1908, Roger Williams University (RWU) entered its final chapter, characterized by a renewed sense of Black autonomy following its relocation and reorganization. The relocation to the White’s Creek Pike campus across the Cumberland River was a symbolic and practical victory for the Tennessee Negro Baptist Association. Under the leadership of Reverend William Haynes, the university reopened on Emancipation Day, January 1, 1908, with a predominantly Black Board of Trustees, signaling a move toward self-determination and away from northern missionary oversight.

The school continued to produce influential graduates, such as Rebecca Jane Perkins Hill (Betty Hill), whose civil rights work in Los Angeles reflected the university’s enduring mission of leadership and social change. Integration of public pools and establishing NAACP chapters were among her many contributions to social progress.

Role Within Nashville’s Black Educational Network

RWU remained part of a "dynamic network" of Black institutions in Nashville, alongside Fisk University, Meharry Medical College, and the Tennessee Agricultural & Industrial State Normal School (now Tennessee State University), contributing to a broader culture of Black higher education.

Mounting Institutional Challenges

Despite the determination of its leaders, the 1920s brought severe challenges. The university struggled with financial pressures, internal denominational divisions, and competition from better-funded state and private institutions. Additionally, the loss of consistent support from the ABHMS and the lack of long-term leadership weakened the institution’s long-term stability.

1929 Merger and Closure

In December 1929, the independent era of Roger Williams University ended when it merged with the Howe Institute in Memphis. The legacy of the institution continues through what is now LeMoyne-Owen College in Memphis, where the remaining students and faculty were integrated.
