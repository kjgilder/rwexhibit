Global Recognition

Roger Williams University reached a pinnacle of international recognition even as it began to face intense external pressures from Nashville’s changing real estate landscape. At the turn of the century, RWU was spotlighted on a global stage through W.E.B. Du Bois and the 1900 Paris Exposition. Portraits of the class of 1899 were featured in Du Bois’s groundbreaking "Exhibit of American Negroes," which aimed to challenge racist stereotypes by showcasing African American intellectual and social progress. This exhibit placed RWU at the heart of a broader narrative of Black achievement, using data visualizations to emphasize the transformative power of teacher training and higher education in the South.

Continued Growth and Impact

However, this period of high visibility was soon met with institutional challenges:

Growth and Recognition

Following the Paris Exposition, the university continued to produce graduates who entered professional fields, including education and ministry, reinforcing its mission as a regional hub for Black leadership.

External Pressures Emerge

By 1904, the university’s thirty-acre campus on Hillsboro Pike had become "prime real estate." Its proximity to the rapidly expanding Vanderbilt University made the land highly desirable to white developers.

Uncertain Future

This desirability led to mounting pressure from local realtors who urged the American Baptist Home Mission Society (ABHMS) to relocate the campus. These external economic forces created an atmosphere of uncertainty for the university’s future just before the catastrophic events of 1905.
