Institutional Crisis and Turning Point

Roger Williams University (RWU) moved from a state of physical devastation to a transformative shift in institutional control during this period. The destruction of the university’s core infrastructure on the Hillsboro Pike campus created an immediate institutional crisis that set the stage for major structural changes.

Student Displacement and Temporary Closure

With its main buildings lost, the school was forced to close temporarily. Students were offered subsidies to transfer to other institutions supported by the American Baptist Home Mission Society (ABHMS), such as Morehouse College in Atlanta.

Land Sales and Racial Exclusion

Rather than rebuilding, the ABHMS capitalized on the land’s high value and proximity to Vanderbilt University. Beginning in 1906, the campus was subdivided and sold for residential use, including racially restrictive covenants that explicitly barred Black ownership of the very land they had developed.

Transition to Black Leadership and Institutional Autonomy

In 1907, a pivotal turning point occurred when Reverend William Haynes and the Tennessee Negro Baptist Association worked with the ABHMS to transfer the university’s incorporation to a predominantly Black Board of Trustees. This transition marked a determined effort by Black leaders to assert greater control over the institution’s future as they planned a new campus at White’s Creek Pike.
