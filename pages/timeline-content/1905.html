Destruction and Crisis

Roger Williams University moved from a state of physical devastation to a transformative shift in institutional control. The year 1905 was marked by two catastrophic fires that destroyed the university's core infrastructure on the Hillsboro Pike campus. The first, on January 24, decimated Centennial Hall, leaving 225 students and several professors without shelter. The second, on May 22, destroyed Mansion House; notably, arson was suspected in this instance due to reports of suspicious noises in the dormitories.

Forced Closure and Dispersal

The aftermath of these disasters led to significant changes: with its main buildings lost, the school was forced to close temporarily. Students were offered subsidies to transfer to other institutions supported by the American Baptist Home Mission Society (ABHMS), such as Morehouse College in Atlanta.

Real Estate Exploitation

Rather than rebuilding, the ABHMS took advantage of the land's high value and proximity to Vanderbilt University. Beginning in 1906, the campus was subdivided and sold for residential use. These sales included racially restrictive covenants that explicitly barred Black ownership of the very land they had developed.

Shift to Black Leadership

In 1907, a pivotal turning point occurred when Reverend William Haynes and the Tennessee Negro Baptist Association worked with the ABHMS to transfer the university’s incorporation to a predominantly Black Board of Trustees. This transition represented a determined effort by Black leaders to take greater autonomy over the institution's future as they planned a new campus at White’s Creek Pike.
